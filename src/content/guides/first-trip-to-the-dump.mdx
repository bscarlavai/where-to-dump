Never been to a landfill or transfer station before? Here's what actually happens: you drive up, you might cross a scale, you unload where they tell you, and you pay based on weight or load size. Most first-timers overthink the process and underthink the loading job. Get the truck packed right and bring the right stuff, and the whole trip takes fifteen minutes.

## What to expect when you get there

### The scale house

Bigger landfills weigh vehicles in and out on a truck scale, and your fee is based on the weight difference, usually priced per ton. You'll pull onto the scale, get a ticket or ticket number, dump your load, then cross the scale again on the way out. The fee gets calculated from that difference. This is standard for larger municipal landfills, less common at small transfer stations.

### Tipping floor vs. open-top containers

At a transfer station, you're often unloading onto a tipping floor, a large concrete area where a loader later pushes everything into a larger trailer for transport to the actual landfill. Other transfer stations use open-top roll-off containers you toss items into directly. Either way, an attendant usually directs you to the right spot. Don't guess and start unloading wherever looks open. If a sorting rule applies (metal in one bin, yard waste in another), they'll generally point it out, but it helps to already have things separated in your truck bed.

## What to bring

- **A photo ID.** Some facilities are residents-only or ask for it to verify a resident discount rate.
- **A tarp and tie-down straps.** Unsecured loads are illegal to drive with in most states, and it's an easy ticket if something falls out on the highway. Cover and strap your load before you leave your driveway, not after a state trooper flags you down.
- **Cash and a card.** Some smaller sites, especially rural transfer stations and county drop-off points, are cash-only. Others are card-only. Bring both so you're not turned away over a payment method.
- **Gloves and boots.** You're handling debris, sometimes sharp or heavy. Closed-toe boots and work gloves make the unloading job faster and safer.

## How to load the truck for a fast unload

The rule is last in, first out. Whatever you load last should be the stuff you want to unload first. If you know you'll be dropping metal in one bin and general debris in another, load the truck in reverse order of how you'll unload it: general debris on the bottom, metal on top, so you're not digging through the whole bed to get to it.

A few practical tips:

1. **Load heavy, flat items first** (appliances, furniture) so they don't shift during the drive and crush lighter stuff.
2. **Keep categories together** rather than mixing everything randomly. It costs you nothing extra at load time and saves real time at the facility.
3. **Leave yourself room to reach items** without climbing fully into the bed. A truck packed to the gills looks efficient but it's miserable to unload alone.

## Sort before you go, it saves money

This is the part most first-timers skip, and it's the biggest cost mistake. A lot of facilities charge less, or nothing, for separated materials:

- **Scrap metal is often free to dump**, sometimes even worth a small payout at a scrap yard instead of the landfill. Mixing metal into your general debris means you pay the general rate on stuff that could've been free.
- **Separated yard waste is usually cheaper than mixed debris**, and sometimes [free entirely](/indiana/accepts/yard-waste/) at a dedicated yard waste site.
- **Tires almost always carry a separate per-tire fee** regardless of what else is in your load, so knowing that going in avoids a surprise. Check what a specific facility charges for [tire disposal](/indiana/accepts/tires/) before you show up with a truck bed full of them.
- **Appliances sometimes have their own fee or their own free program** depending on whether they contain refrigerants. Check [appliance rules](/indiana/accepts/appliances/) before assuming they're covered under a general fee.

Fees vary a lot by facility, so treat any number you hear as typical rather than guaranteed. Fifteen minutes of sorting before you leave the house can turn a $60 dump trip into a $20 one.

## Finding the right place

Not every facility takes every material, and prices vary more than people expect for what feels like a commodity service. Search [near you](/near-me/) or browse your [state's facilities](/states/) before you load up, so you're not stuck driving across town because the first place you tried doesn't take what you're hauling.

## Before you go

Call ahead if you've got anything unusual in the load (electronics, tires, appliances, anything that might need a separate fee or a separate facility entirely). Fees and accepted materials change, so don't rely on what a friend told you six months ago. Bring ID in case the site checks residency, and bring both cash and a card since you won't always know which one the site takes until you're standing at the booth.
