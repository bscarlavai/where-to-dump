Short answer: yes. Most transfer stations and landfills will take your old mattress, and you'll typically pay somewhere between **$10 and $25 per piece**. Box springs usually carry their own separate fee on top of that, so a full mattress-and-box-spring set can run you $20 to $50 depending on the facility.

That's more than you'd pay to toss a bag of regular trash, and there's a reason for it.

## Why mattresses cost extra

Mattresses are a headache for facility operators, plain and simple. The steel springs and coils inside don't compact. Feed one into a compactor built for household trash and you risk snagging the ram, puncturing the walls, or jamming the whole machine until someone has to climb in and cut it free. Box springs are worse: they're basically a wooden frame wrapped around a spring lattice, and shapes like that have a habit of tangling in loader equipment and conveyor belts.

So facilities charge a handling fee to cover the extra labor of pulling mattresses aside and dealing with them separately, often by hand-loading them into a dedicated roll-off instead of running them through the compactor. It's not a punishment, it's just what it costs to process something oddly shaped and full of metal.

Some landfills also charge more if the mattress is soiled, torn open, or shows signs of bed bugs (a real reason some facilities quote a higher fee or refuse an item over the phone). If yours is in rough shape, mention that when you call.

## Size matters too

Fees usually creep up with size. A twin mattress is cheaper to process than a king, simply because it's smaller and lighter, and some facilities post separate rates for twin/full versus queen/king. If you've got a bunk bed set or a couple of twin mattresses from a kid's room, that's often cheaper per piece than a single king from the primary bedroom, so don't assume "one mattress" always costs the same as "another mattress."

Foundations (the low-profile alternative to a box spring, usually a solid or slatted wood platform instead of springs) sometimes get treated differently too. Some facilities charge the standard box spring rate, others charge less since there's no metal coil lattice to snag equipment. Worth asking when you call.

## What you'll actually pay

Typical pricing for a single mattress or box spring runs:

- **$10 to $25** per piece at most transfer stations and landfills
- Some facilities charge **per mattress and per box spring separately**, so a full set can hit $40 to $50
- A few smaller county sites fold mattress disposal into a flat load fee instead of charging per item

These are typical ranges, not guarantees. Fees vary by facility and by state, so [find a facility near you](/near-me/) and call ahead to confirm before you load up the truck.

## Cheaper (or free) alternatives

Before you pay a landfill fee, a few options are worth checking first.

**Retailer take-back.** If you're buying a new mattress, ask the retailer about haul-away service when it's delivered. Most major mattress and furniture retailers will remove your old one for free or a small fee, sometimes bundled right into the delivery charge. This is the easiest route if the timing works out.

**Donation.** Clean mattresses in decent shape, no stains, no tears, no odor, can sometimes go to shelters, thrift stores, or charities that furnish apartments for people in need. Not every charity accepts mattresses because of bed bug liability, so call ahead, but it's worth asking before you assume the dump is your only option.

**Mattress recyclers.** A growing number of specialty recyclers break mattresses down into their component materials, steel, foam, wood, and fabric, and either charge less than a landfill or take them free where mattress recycling laws exist. A handful of states mandate recycling programs with free drop-off, so it's worth a quick search for "mattress recycling" plus your city before you default to the dump.

## Where to take it

Most standard transfer stations and landfills accept mattresses, but not every small recycling center does, since handling them properly takes dedicated space and staff. Before you drive across town with a mattress strapped to your roof, check what a facility actually accepts. Browse [facilities by state](/states/) to find contact info and hours for sites near you.

If you're already doing a bigger cleanout, hauling old furniture or [construction debris](/indiana/accepts/construction-debris/) alongside the mattress, it's worth checking a single facility's full list of accepted materials rather than making separate trips.

## Getting it there in one piece

A queen or king mattress is awkward to move solo, and it acts like a giant sail if you strap it to a roof rack. Wrap it in plastic sheeting if it's going to ride outside, both to keep it from soaking up rain (a wet mattress is heavier, messier, and some facilities will charge more for a soaked one) and to keep debris from blowing off it onto the highway. Use ratchet straps, not bungee cords, across at least two points. If you don't have a truck or trailer, some facilities and haulers offer pickup for a flat fee that often beats the hassle of borrowing a truck for an afternoon.

## Before you go

Call the facility first. Not every site handles mattresses, and the ones that do may quote different prices depending on condition, size, or whether it's soiled. Bring ID if the site asks for one, and keep cash on hand since some smaller facilities don't run cards. And if you're headed to a county-run site, confirm it isn't resident-only before you make the drive, plenty of them are.
