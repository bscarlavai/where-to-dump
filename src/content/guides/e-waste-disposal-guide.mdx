Old TV, dead laptop, a drawer full of cables you'll never use again: none of it belongs in your regular trash. Most electronics can go to a dedicated e-waste drop-off site or a retailer take-back program for free. The exception is anything with a picture tube. CRT TVs and monitors commonly run **$5 to $25** to drop off because of the leaded glass inside. Everything else is usually free.

## Why electronics can't just go in the trash

Circuit boards, batteries, and screens carry lead, mercury, and cadmium. In a landfill, that stuff doesn't just sit there quietly. It can leach into soil and groundwater as the casing breaks down over years. That's why a growing list of states have banned electronics from landfills outright, and why plenty of transfer stations that technically could take them choose not to.

If your hauler or transfer station rejects a TV or a box of old phones, this is why. It's not arbitrary. Find a facility near you that handles e-waste specifically before you load up the truck, because not every transfer station does.

## Where e-waste actually goes

### Dedicated e-waste drop-off sites

Most metro areas and a lot of counties have a recycler that specializes in electronics. These are usually the cheapest and most reliable option for bulk stuff (old office equipment, a pile of monitors, that kind of thing). Search [/near-me/](/near-me/) or your [state page](/states/) to find one close to you.

### County collection events

Plenty of counties run e-waste collection days a few times a year, often paired with document shredding or household hazardous waste collection. These are usually free and sometimes the only option in rural areas without a permanent drop-off site. Check your county's solid waste website or call ahead, since dates move around year to year.

### Retailer take-back programs

Best Buy takes most consumer electronics at customer service, no purchase necessary, and it's free for the vast majority of items. There are daily limits on some categories, so don't show up with a truck bed full of monitors and expect them to take all of it at once. Staples also takes ink, toner, and select electronics. Both are solid options if you've just got a few items and don't want to hunt down a specialized recycler.

### Manufacturer mail-back programs

Apple, Dell, and several other manufacturers will mail you a prepaid shipping label or box for old devices, particularly their own brand. This is slower than dropping something off, but it's genuinely free and convenient if you're only dealing with one or two small items like a phone or a laptop.

### Scrap and metal recyclers

If you've got a real pile of old electronics (a dead server rack, a box of hard drives, several years of retired office gear), a scrap metal recycler will sometimes take it, and might even pay you a small amount for the copper and aluminum inside. This is more of a bulk-cleanout option than a one-off drop, but worth a call if you're clearing out a garage or a small office.

## What it actually costs

- **TVs and CRT monitors**: commonly **$5 to $25** per unit, because of the leaded glass in the tube. Flat-screen TVs sometimes cost less than old tube TVs, or nothing at all, depending on the facility.
- **Computers, laptops, printers, small electronics**: usually **free** at dedicated e-waste sites and retailer programs.
- **Large appliances with electronic components** (like a smart fridge): these often fall under [appliance disposal](/indiana/accepts/appliances/) rules rather than e-waste, so check which category your facility uses before hauling it over.

Fees vary quite a bit by facility and by state, so treat these as typical ranges, not guarantees. A quick call before you load the truck will save you a wasted trip.

## Wipe your data first

Before anything leaves your hands, do this:

1. **Factory reset** phones, tablets, and computers. Go through the actual settings menu reset, not just deleting files, since deleted files aren't really gone.
2. **Pull the hard drive** out of desktops and old laptops if you're not confident the reset wiped everything, or if it's a work computer that had sensitive data. Keep the drive, destroy it yourself, or bring it separately to a shredding event.
3. **Log out of accounts** and remove SIM cards from phones before they leave your possession.
4. **Check external drives and old USB sticks** for anything you forgot about, especially tax documents or photos.

Recyclers generally aren't in the business of digging through your data, but a factory reset takes five minutes and it's one less thing to worry about.

## Before you go

Call the site first. Hours and accepted-item lists change, and some e-waste events are limited to residents of that county only. Bring a photo ID if it's a residency-restricted site. If you've got a mixed load with appliances, tires, or yard debris along with the electronics, sort it in the truck bed first: mixed loads take longer to unload and some facilities will turn you away rather than sort it for you.
