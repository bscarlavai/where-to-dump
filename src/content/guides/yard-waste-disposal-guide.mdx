Grass clippings and brush usually don't belong in the regular landfill pile, and in a lot of states they're banned there outright. The good news is a municipal compost or yard-waste drop-off site is often free for residents, and it's usually the fastest option anyway since you skip the tipping fee scale entirely. Here's where it goes and what actually counts as yard waste.

## Why yard waste gets banned from landfills

Leaves, grass, and brush are compostable. Buried in a landfill under tons of other trash, they don't get the air they need to break down cleanly. Instead they decompose anaerobically and produce methane, a greenhouse gas that's considerably more potent than carbon dioxide over the short term. That's the whole reason a growing number of states passed yard waste landfill bans starting back in the 1990s: it's a waste stream that composts easily on its own, so burying it is just wasteful.

Practically, this means a lot of landfills and transfer stations either won't take yard waste at all or charge more for it than they would for a load of compostable material dropped at a dedicated yard waste site. Check your [state page](/states/) or find a facility [near you](/near-me/) that's set up for it specifically.

## Where to take it

### Municipal compost or yard-waste drop-off

Most cities and a lot of counties run a dedicated yard waste site, separate from the regular landfill or transfer station. These are commonly **free for residents**, sometimes with a proof-of-residency requirement (a driver's license with a local address usually does it). Non-residents sometimes pay a per-load fee, commonly in the **$5 to $20** range depending on load size. This is close to always your cheapest option if one exists near you, so check before assuming you have to pay tipping fees like you would for regular trash.

### Curbside seasonal pickup

A lot of municipalities offer curbside yard waste pickup, especially in fall for leaves and spring for general cleanup. This is usually included in your regular trash service or a small add-on fee, and it's the least effort option if your pile fits within the pickup limits (usually bagged leaves or bundled brush under a certain length). Check your city's sanitation calendar, since these programs are often seasonal rather than year-round.

### Burning: check local rules first

Burning yard waste is legal in a lot of rural areas but the rules vary enormously by county and even by time of year (burn bans during dry seasons are common). Some counties require a permit, some ban burning within city limits entirely, and some allow it only on certain days or with size restrictions on the pile. Don't assume what worked at your last address applies here. A five-minute call to your county's fire department or extension office will tell you exactly what's allowed.

### Hire it out vs. DIY

For a big cleanup (storm damage, an overgrown lot, a full tree removal), hiring a tree service or junk hauler to deal with the debris is often worth the cost, especially if you don't have a trailer or truck rated for the load. A pickup with a couple of loaded trips is usually cheaper if you've got the vehicle and the time. The break-even point is usually somewhere around a full trailer load: below that, DIY tends to win on cost; above it, your time and gas start eating into the savings.

## What counts as yard waste

- Leaves
- Brush and limbs, usually up to a **diameter limit** (commonly 4 to 6 inches, though this varies a lot by facility, so check first)
- Grass clippings
- Garden trimmings and plant debris

## What does not count

- Dirt and soil
- Sod (often handled separately due to weight and disposal rules)
- Rocks and stone
- Treated or painted lumber (this usually needs to go through [construction debris](/indiana/accepts/construction-debris/) disposal instead, since treated wood carries chemical preservatives that yard waste composting sites won't accept)

Mixing these into a yard waste load is one of the more common ways to get turned away at the gate, so sort before you load the truck rather than after you arrive.

## A quick note on invasive plants and diseased material

If you're clearing out something like poison ivy, an invasive vine, or a tree that came down with a disease (oak wilt and similar issues are the usual culprits), some facilities want that bagged separately or handled through a different process entirely, since composting it can spread the problem rather than solve it. This isn't universal, but it's worth a call if you know that's what you're dealing with. Most routine leaf and brush piles don't need to worry about this at all.

## Before you go

Call the site to confirm hours and whether it's residents-only, since a lot of yard waste sites restrict access to people who live in that city or county. Bring ID if residency matters. Diameter limits on brush and per-load fees both vary by facility and by season, so don't assume last year's rules still apply.
