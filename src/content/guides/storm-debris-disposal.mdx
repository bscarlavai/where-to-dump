After a big storm, the rules change. Landfills that normally charge waive fees for a few weeks. Counties open temporary debris sites in parking lots and fairgrounds. Curbside crews come through on schedules nobody can quite pin down. It's the one time disposal gets easier and more confusing at the same time.

Here's how it actually works, so you can get a yard full of branches (or worse) gone without paying for it or getting a citation.

## The first 48 hours: don't haul yet

Right after a declared storm event, hold off on loading the truck. Two reasons:

- **Fee waivers take a few days to announce.** Most counties waive landfill tipping fees for storm debris, but the announcement usually comes 2 to 5 days after the event, once officials assess damage. Haul on day one and you pay full price for a load that would've been free on Friday.
- **Curbside pickup may be coming to you.** After federally declared disasters, and plenty of state-level ones, municipalities run debris removal routes. If a truck with a claw is coming anyway, your only job is stacking it right.

Check your county emergency management page and the county or city social feeds. That's where waivers and pickup schedules get posted first, usually before the news covers it.

## Sorting matters more than usual

Storm debris crews and drop-off sites almost always require separated piles. Mixed piles get skipped by curbside crews and turned away at drop-offs. The standard categories:

- **Vegetative debris**: branches, limbs, brush, whole shrubs. Usually the free-est category and often ground into mulch.
- **Construction debris**: shingles, fencing, siding, drywall, lumber with nails. Free during a waiver window, charged after it.
- **Appliances and metal**: fridges, water heaters, sheet metal. Kept separate because of refrigerants and scrap value.
- **Household hazardous waste**: paint, chemicals, propane tanks, gas cans. Never goes in a debris pile. Watch for special post-storm HHW collection events.
- **Electronics**: TVs and computers, separate at most sites.

For curbside: stack piles at the edge of your property near the road, not on the pavement, not under power lines, not on top of a water meter or hydrant, and not touching the mailbox post. Crews measure by the cubic yard with a claw arm; anything they can't grab cleanly gets left.

## Temporary debris sites

For bigger events, counties open temporary collection sites so the landfill line doesn't back up onto the highway. They're usually at fairgrounds, park-and-rides, or closed transfer stations, and they take vegetative and construction debris for free with proof of residency.

These sites are announced, opened, and closed fast, sometimes within three weeks. The county emergency management page lists them; so do local news roundups. Bring your license: nearly every temporary site checks residency, because out-of-county contractors will absolutely fill a free site with paid-job debris otherwise.

## The contractor rule that catches people

If you hire someone to cut up the tree or tear off the damaged roof, in most counties **their debris doesn't qualify for free disposal or curbside pickup**. Contractor-generated debris is commercial waste, and the contractor is expected to haul it and pay disposal on it (it's built into what they charge you).

Some homeowners "help" a contractor by dragging the debris to the curb as their own. If the county notices a roofing job's worth of shingles in your pile, expect the pile to be skipped and possibly a code enforcement letter. During a declared disaster, some counties relax this. Check before you assume.

## After the waiver window closes

Fee waivers end quietly. Once they do, storm debris is just regular debris:

- **Vegetative debris** goes to your normal yard waste options, which stay cheap or free for residents at many facilities year-round.
- **Construction debris** pays standard C&D rates at the landfill or transfer station, typically $40 to $80 a ton.
- **A tree service** is often worth it for anything still standing but damaged. Removal is dangerous work, and dropping a widow-maker limb on your own is how ERs meet homeowners.

Every facility in this directory lists what it accepts, and many list fees. Look up your county's landfills and transfer stations, check the residency rules, and call before hauling anything unusual. After a storm, hours change without warning and lines run long, so an early morning trip midweek beats Saturday every time.
