Old appliances, what the industry calls "white goods," are often cheaper to get rid of than you'd think, sometimes free. The catch is refrigerant. Anything that ever held Freon or a modern refrigerant needs that gas professionally recovered before the unit can be scrapped, and that's where the fees come from.

## The free (or paying) option: scrap yards

Washers, dryers, dishwashers, stoves, and water heaters are mostly steel, and steel has resale value. Scrap yards want that metal, so plenty of them will take these appliances **for free**, and some will actually pay you a few dollars per unit depending on current scrap prices and how much the appliance weighs.

This only applies to appliances without refrigerant. A dryer or a stove is just metal and wiring, easy to process, no regulatory hassle. That's why it's often the cheapest disposal route for those items, cheaper than a landfill or transfer station in most cases.

## The appliances that cost money: anything with refrigerant

Refrigerators, freezers, window and central air conditioners, and dehumidifiers all contain refrigerant, and federal law (the Clean Air Act) prohibits venting it into the atmosphere. Before one of these can be scrapped or landfilled, a certified technician has to recover the refrigerant with specialized equipment.

That labor and equipment is exactly why these appliances cost more to drop off. Typical fees run **$20 to $160**, depending on the facility, the unit's size, and whether the refrigerant has already been removed (a unit with a certification tag showing the refrigerant was already recovered will usually cost less, or nothing, to drop off).

If you're not sure whether an appliance still has refrigerant in it, assume it does unless you have documentation saying otherwise. Facilities generally can't take your word for it either, since they're the ones liable for handling it correctly. Check what a specific site requires by looking up [what's accepted near you](/indiana/accepts/appliances/) before hauling a fridge across town.

## Older units aren't always worth saving

If your fridge or freezer is more than 15 years old, running it costs more in electricity over a few years than most rebate programs pay out, so don't let the rebate check talk you into keeping a second beer fridge in the garage "since it still works." Utilities offer these programs specifically because an old compressor sitting in a garage or basement, often running non-stop with a poor door seal, is a meaningfully worse deal for the grid than a new ENERGY STAR unit. If a unit is more of a burden than a convenience, the rebate is the push to finally let it go rather than a reason to keep an old one running longer.

## Utility rebate programs

Before you pay a landfill fee for an old fridge or freezer, check whether your electric utility runs a recycling rebate program. Many utilities will pick up an old, working refrigerator or freezer from your home for free and pay you **$30 to $75** for it, on top of the free removal. The logic is simple: an old fridge running in a garage or basement is a bigger drain on the grid than a new one, so utilities subsidize getting rid of them.

This is genuinely one of the best deals in appliance disposal if your unit still runs. It costs you nothing, they haul it away, and you get paid for the trouble. Search your utility's name plus "refrigerator recycling rebate" to see if they run one; coverage varies a lot by region and isn't universal.

## What about window AC units and dehumidifiers

These get overlooked because they're small enough to feel like they shouldn't need special handling, but they hold refrigerant just like a full-size fridge does, and the same rules apply. Don't put a window AC unit or dehumidifier out with bulk trash pickup or drop it at a facility that doesn't handle refrigerant recovery. Scrap yards that don't have a certified technician on staff will often turn these away for the same reason they'd turn away a fridge, even though the unit looks small and simple. Call ahead just like you would for a full-size refrigerator.

## Retailer haul-away

If you're replacing an appliance, ask the retailer about haul-away service when the new one gets delivered. Most major appliance retailers will remove your old unit as part of delivery, often for free or a modest fee, and they're already set up to handle refrigerant units properly. This skips the drive to a facility entirely, hard to beat if the timing works out.

## Putting it together

A rough order of operations, cheapest to most expensive:

1. **Utility rebate pickup**, if it's a working fridge or freezer: free removal, and they pay you
2. **Retailer haul-away** on delivery of the replacement: usually free or low cost
3. **Scrap yard drop-off** for non-refrigerant units like stoves, dryers, and washers: often free, sometimes they pay
4. **Transfer station or landfill** for refrigerant units without another option: typically $20 to $160 depending on the unit and facility

If you're clearing out multiple appliances at once, say from a kitchen remodel, it's worth splitting the load: non-refrigerant units to the scrap yard, refrigerant units to a facility that handles recovery. One stop isn't always the cheapest stop, and [finding a facility near you](/near-me/) that handles both saves you a second trip.

## Before you go

Call the facility and ask directly whether they handle refrigerant recovery on site, not every transfer station does, and some will turn you away at the gate if they don't. Bring any documentation showing refrigerant was already recovered if you have it; it can lower or waive the fee. If you're going the scrap yard route, call first to confirm they're taking appliances that day and ask what ID they require, some log sellers for regulatory reasons. And if a cash payout is on the table, don't expect a check: most scrap yards pay cash on the spot.
