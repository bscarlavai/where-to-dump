Nobody posts their prices online, so you show up not knowing if you owe $12 or $120. Here's how dump pricing actually works, plus the typical ranges facilities charge, so you can budget before you load the truck.

## Per-ton pricing (the big sites)

Larger landfills and transfer stations weigh your load on a scale, both coming in and going out, and charge you for the difference. You drive onto the scale loaded, dump your load, then drive back over the scale empty. The gate fee is based on the weight you left behind.

Typical per-ton rates run **$30 to $80**, though some facilities, especially ones in areas with limited landfill capacity or higher hauling costs, charge as much as **$175 per ton**. A pickup bed of mixed household junk usually weighs somewhere between a quarter ton and a full ton depending on what's in it, so do the math before you assume a "per ton" quote sounds cheap.

Most scale facilities also apply a **minimum charge**, commonly **$15 to $80**, so even a small load gets billed at least that much regardless of actual weight. If you've only got a trunk full of stuff, you're likely paying the minimum, not a true per-ton rate.

## Per-load and per-cubic-yard pricing (smaller sites)

Smaller transfer stations, especially rural or county-run ones, often skip the scale entirely and charge by load size instead. Common structures:

- **Flat per-load pricing** for a car or small pickup load, commonly **$2 to $30**
- **Per cubic yard** pricing for bigger loads, typically **$17 to $30 per cubic yard**, with staff eyeballing (or measuring) how much space your junk takes up in the truck bed or trailer

This method is faster for everyone, no scale required, but it's less precise. A truck bed packed tight with dense debris like concrete will effectively cost you more per pound than one loosely filled with bulky, light stuff like furniture, even though the visual "load size" charge looks the same either way.

## Per-item surcharges

On top of whatever base pricing method a facility uses, certain items carry their own flat surcharge because they're a pain to process. Think [tires](/indiana/accepts/tires/), mattresses, appliances with refrigerant, and large furniture. These fees stack on top of your gate fee, they're not included in it. A load of construction debris that also has a couple of old tires tossed in gets charged for the debris by weight or volume, plus a separate per-tire fee.

If you're hauling a mix of stuff, say [construction debris](/indiana/accepts/construction-debris/) and yard waste and a busted appliance, ask the attendant to break down what's being charged for what. It's not unusual for one load to hit three different pricing rules at once.

## A quick example

Say you're clearing out a garage: a broken lawn mower, some scrap lumber, a few bags of old paint cans (which some facilities won't take at all, more on that below), and a couple of bald tires. At a scale facility, all of that gets weighed together and billed at the per-ton rate, with the tires broken out as a separate per-item surcharge. At a smaller per-load site, the attendant might eyeball the truck bed, quote you a flat load price, then add the tire surcharges on top.

Either way, the tires and any hazardous items (paint, batteries, chemicals) usually get pulled out and billed or handled separately from the bulk of the load. If you're not sure whether something in your load counts as hazardous, ask before you unload rather than after, some facilities won't let you take a rejected item back off the scale for free once it's been weighed in.

## Cash vs. card

Plenty of smaller and county-run facilities are cash-only, or cash-preferred with a card surcharge. Don't assume you can swipe a card and drive off. Bring cash, especially if you're headed to a rural transfer station rather than a larger regional landfill. The bigger commercial sites are more likely to take cards without a fuss.

## Resident discounts

County and municipal sites frequently charge a lower rate, or nothing at all, for residents of that county or township, and a noticeably higher rate for everyone else. Some go further and simply refuse non-residents outright. If you're near a county line, check whether the closer facility is actually open to you before you drive there expecting to dump.

This is one of the most common surprises people run into: you get weighed in, dump the load, and find out the rate at the scale house is double what you expected because you don't live in that county. [Search facilities near you](/near-me/) to see which sites in your area are open to the public versus resident-only.

## Why the pricing isn't posted online

Part of the reason gate fees are so hard to find ahead of time is that they shift with disposal costs upstream. A facility's own tipping fee at the landfill it hauls to, fuel costs, and how full its permitted capacity is can all push rates up or down over the course of a year. Smaller sites in particular tend to update their price sheet at the gate rather than on a website that someone has to remember to touch. That's the practical reason to call first rather than trust a number you found somewhere online, including this one.

## Before you go

Fees change more often than facilities update their websites, so call ahead and confirm current pricing, especially if you're hauling a full trailer. Ask specifically about minimum charges and any per-item surcharges for what you're bringing. If it's a county site, ask whether you need ID or proof of residency at the gate, and bring cash just in case the card reader happens to be down that day.
